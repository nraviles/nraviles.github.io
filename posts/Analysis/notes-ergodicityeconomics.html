Introduction

Fundamentally, agents must act in and react to the environment they inhabit. If in this the system dynamics of scale matter, they should accordingly take action in a way that differentiate between scales; if time matters they choose their actions under the precedence that they must live or die in time. This is rudimentary, and the models (and actions) of in this context need to be coherent with this.

This is not what standard Economics does explicitly, but it is what it envisioned. Ergodicity Economics is one attempt to clarify this goal, in studying actors under varying dynamics and proscribing model frameworks (for the part that is an experimental and not just formal science) which account especially for inherent temporal components.

Path-wise Dynamics

Ensembles vs. Paths

To provide context we will discuss briefly the difference between ensembles and paths. Consider the simple random walk \((\Sigma_1,\Sigma_2,\dots)\), with \(\Sigma_n \sum_{i=1}^n S_i\) where \(S_i\) is either \(-1\) or \(1\) with equal probability. An ensemble, or population, refers to a set of resamples of a random varibles, i.e. \((\Sigma_n^{(1)},\Sigma_n^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_n^{(m)})\) such that each \(\Sigma_n^{(j)}\) for \(j\in[m]\) marginally has the same distribution.

These \(\Sigma_n^{(j)}\) need not be independent, ensembles often just refer to a “sample” of random variables that are exchangeable. Specifically we assume that the distribution of the sample vector is invariant under any permutation \(\pi\) of \([m]\) \[ \begin{equation*} (\Sigma_n^{(1)},\Sigma_n^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_n^{(m)}) \deq (\Sigma_n^{(\pi(1))},\Sigma_n^{(\pi(2))},\dots, \Sigma_n^{(\pi(m))})\doteq\cbr{\Sigma_n^{(j)}}_{j=1}^m. \end{equation*} \] An important specification is \(n\), which acts as the time parameter. In the above definition, what was “scrambled” was the index associated with each value at time \(n\), not \(n\) itself. It is clear therefore (given the structure of \(\Sigma_n^{(m)}\)) that a temporal permutation, both within the ensemble, and within a specific sample is not invariant: the (ordered) vector1 \[ \begin{equation*} (\Sigma_{1}^{(j)},\Sigma_{2}^{(j)},\dots, \Sigma_{n}^{(j)}) \not \deq (\Sigma_{\pi(1)}^{(j)},\Sigma_{\pi(2)}^{(j)},\dots, \Sigma_{\pi(n)}^{(j)}). \end{equation*} \]

1 Clearly the distribution of a simple random walk is such that \(\Sigma_{n}^{(j)}\) and \(\Sigma_{n+k}^{(j)}\) are related, differing only by \(\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+k}S_i\); a time dependence. Invariance in permutation of label within a sample, so as to be considered an ensemble, just means that label doesn’t affect the distribution (but it might depend on the sample size \(m\)!). Similarly invariance to permutation to time identifies that time doesn’t affect the distribution.

2 a simple random walk is not invariant to time, unless the walk is on a “finite graph”; e.g., a reflected walk with bounds at \(\abs{\Sigma_k} = B\) (constraining \(\abs{\Sigma_k} \in [B]\) for all \(k\in \Z_+\)).

This leads to the question of what would it mean for an ensemble to be invariant to time?2

If a random variable was invariant to time, each of the ensembles are also: \[ \begin{equation*} (\Sigma_{n}^{(1)},\Sigma_{n}^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_{n}^{(m)}) \deq (\Sigma_{\pi(n)}^{(1)},\Sigma_{\pi(n)}^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_{\pi(n)}^{(m)}). \end{equation*} \] A strengthening of this would be an invariance under compositions of single component permutations \[ \begin{equation*} (\Sigma_{n}^{(1)},\Sigma_{n}^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_{n}^{(m)}) \deq (\Sigma_{\pi(n)}^{(1)},\Sigma_{n}^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_{n}^{(m)}), \end{equation*} \] into multiple component-wise permutations \(\pi_j\) \[ \begin{equation*} (\Sigma_{n}^{(1)},\Sigma_{n}^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_{n}^{(m)}) \deq (\Sigma_{\pi_1(n)}^{(1)},\Sigma_{\pi_2(n)}^{(2)},\dots, \Sigma_{\pi_2(n)}^{(m)}). \end{equation*} \] A further strengthening requires “consistency” of invariance under permutations: we must extend our definition of exchangeability to invariance under permutations \(\pi\) of \([\infty]\).

Clearly as mentioned previously, for a simple random walk, for \(\pi_n\) a permutation of \([n]\) \[ \begin{equation*} (\Sigma_{1}^{(j)},\Sigma_{2}^{(j)},\dots, \Sigma_{n}^{(j)}) \not \deq (\Sigma_{\pi(1)}^{(j)},\Sigma_{\pi(2)}^{(j)},\dots, \Sigma_{\pi(n)}^{(j)}). \end{equation*} \] It is also therefore not invariant under \(\pi_\infty\) a permutation of \(\infty\). What is however, are the increments of a simple random walk \[ \begin{equation*} (S_{1}^{(j)},S_{2}^{(j)},\dots, S_{n}^{(j)}) \deq (S_{\pi_\infty(1)}^{(j)},S_{\pi_\infty(2)}^{(j)},\dots, S_{\pi_\infty(n)}^{(j)}). \end{equation*} \] Here, we can also permute the index \[ \begin{equation*} (S_{1}^{(j)},S_{2}^{(j)},\dots, S_{n}^{(j)}) \deq (S_{\pi_\infty(1)}^{(\pi(j))},S_{\pi_\infty(2)}^{(\pi(j))},\dots, S_{\pi_\infty(n)}^{(\pi(j))}). \end{equation*} \] For instance consider a random path \(\pr{X_t^{(1)}}_{t\ge 0}\) and an ensemble at an arbitrary time \(\tau\), \(\cbr{X_\tau^{(j)}}_{j\ge 1}\), where invariance under \(\pi_\infty\) holds in both time and label. Then for bounded functions \(f\) (or potentially a larger class)

\[ \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f\pr{X_t^{(1)}} \deq \frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T}f\pr{X_\tau^{(j)}}. \end{equation*} \] This will be the primary observation of Ergodic Theory, providing conditions for the weaker result that that we might asymptotically (eventually past a certain point in time) the ensemble and temporal are exchangeable, where for all choices of bounded \(f\) conditioned on convergence (via the strong law of large numbers) \[ \begin{equation*} \lim_{T\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}f\pr{X_t^{(1)}} = \lim_{T\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=0}^{T}f\pr{X_\tau^{(j)}} = \E[f(X_1)], \quad \mathrm{a.s}. \end{equation*} \]

Ergodicity Concepts

Ergodicity gives us tools to relate ensemble and path-wise dynamics, but is the weakest in the mixing hierarchy: \(\mathrm{mixing} \implies \mathrm{(weak)mixing} \implies \mathrm{ergodic}\). We will briefly run through relevant definitions, which we sometimes specialize for easy of discussion here.

Definition 1 For a measure space \((\Omega,\mu)\) for \(\Omega\) a Banach space and \(\mu\) a measure over \(\sigma(\Omega)\), we call \(T_s : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega\) for \(s\in \R_+\) the transform map of a random variable \(X_s(\omega)\) for \(\omega \in \Omega\) if:

  1. \(T\) is measure preserving: \(\mu(T^{-1}(\Omega)) = \mu(\Omega)\).
  2. \(T_s\) is measureable for all \(s\), i.e. for \(A\in \sigma(\Omega)\), \(T_s^{-1}(A) \in \sigma(\Omega)\), where we write \((\mu\circ T_s^{-1})(A) = \mu_s(A) = \Pr[X_s \in A]\).
  3. \(T_s\) admits composition \(T_s \circ T_{t} = T_{s+t}\)
  4. \(T_S\) is continuous in \(s\), i.e. \(\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\norm{T_s - T_{s+h}} =0\)

Definition 2 We say that \(T\), a transform map is (strongly) mixing for measure \(\mu\) if \[ \begin{equation*} \lim_{t\uparrow\infty}\mu(A \cap T_t^{-1}(B)) = \mu(A)\mu(B) \end{equation*} \]

Definition 3 We say that \(T\), a transform map is weakly mixing for measure \(\mu\) if \[ \begin{equation*} \lim_{N\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\abs{\mu(A \cap T_k^{-1}(B)) -\mu(A)\mu(B)} = 0 \end{equation*} \]

Definition 4 We say that \(T\), a transform map is ergodic for measure \(\mu\) if:

  1. \(T\) is measure preserving: \(\mu(T^{-1}(\Omega)) = \mu(\Omega)\)
  2. If \(J\) is an invariant map of \(T\), i.e. \(T^{-1}(J) = J\), then \(\mu(J) \in \cbr{0,1}\).

This statement implies that \(J\) must be either “essentially” the entire space (\(\mu(\Omega) = 1\)) or “essentially” nothing (\(\mu(\phi) = 0\)); in either case the difference between \(J\) and the two options is of size or measure zero - e.g. maybe a countable number of points for example.3

3 This, as already indicated, identifies that once a random variable \(X_s \in J\) (associated to our probability measure \(\mu_s\)), it is in there forever with probability 1: if \(\mu(T_s^{-1}J) = 0\) then almost all \(X_s\) with probability \(1\) are never in \(J\) if initialized with distribution \(\mu\) (note \(T_0\) is the identify map by the continuity assumption). This is just a reiteration of the invariance under the forward map, \(\Pr[X_s \in J] = \mu(T_s^{-1}J) = \mu(J)\in \cbr{0,1}\), plus the measure preserving property and definition of ergodicity.

We note that ergodic random variables \(X_s\) (those constructed via an ergodic map: \(X_{s+t} = T_t (X_s)\)), by construction identify \(\mu\) as their stationary distribtion, i.e. \(\lim_{t\uparrow\infty}\mu \circ T_s^{-1} = \mu\) where instead simply \(\mu_s = \mu_t\) for all \(s,t\ge 0\). Ergodicity is a stricter form of stationarity (finer). The distiction needed to be made here is the exact initial position \(X_s\).

We will assume that \(X_0(\omega)=\omega\), i.e. we do not start observing all paths, observable in aggregate with \(\mu\), but observe the specific process \(\pr{X_s(\omega)}_{s \ge 0}\) with distribution path \[ \mu_s^{\omega}\doteq\pr{\mu_s\circ \delta_\omega}_{s \ge 0} \] which is not stationary. Written this way, it is still deceiving, as the distribution path \(\mu_s^{\omega}\) is still about an ensemble given a starting position, not the path \(X_s(\omega)\) itself (which may be completely determined by an individual \(\omega\)).4

4 In the previous section’s discussion, we should note that stationarity and ergodicity in time are equivalent to invariance of \((\Sigma_k)_{k\in \Z}\) under displacement in time \(T_n(\Sigma_k)_{k\in \Z} = (\Sigma_{k+n})_{k\in \Z} \deq (\Sigma_{k})_{k\in \Z}\). Here we are again with respect to the simple random walk, with all its other problems, we have a non-stationary process where \(\Sigma_0 = 0\), i.e. \(\mu_k^{0}\).

5 That \(X\sim \mu\) further stipulates that we cannot look at any ensemble initialized on say \(A\), with corresponding measure path \(\mu_s^{A}\) but that we also need to wait long enough that \(\mu_s^A \approx \mu\). This begs the question of whether the current ensemble, potentially as it evolves, is a good representative sample to measure from in order to estimate our paths, as in practice (imagining \(A\) as a discrete set of points; a physical population) we estimate \[ \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}f\pr{X_t(\omega)} \approx \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\E[f(X_t^A)] \approx \E[f(X)]. \end{equation*} \] The stipulation here is that eventually all paths and the ensemble “look” alike, you could even “substitute” one for the other as representative for the same individual at different point in their life.

The primary observation of Ergodic theory is that there is information about all individual paths observable (measurable) from the ensemble, if we watch the ensemble for a long enough time. Here, we have what is called Birkoff’s theorem, indicating that for almost any initialization of \(X_s(\omega)\) (with associated measure and ergodic map) \[ \begin{equation*} \lim_{T\uparrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}f\pr{X_t} = \E[f(X)], \quad \mathrm{a.s}. \end{equation*} \] where \(X\) identifies the distributional limit of \(X_t\), or rather \(X\sim \mu\), for stationary measure \(\mu\). Here the identification that this result is almost sure is why this is often called the “Point-wise Ergodic Theorem”, which say for almost all but a size 0 set of paths, the time average converges to a deterministic value corresponding to the ensemble average for the function (generalization of law of large numbers). 5

As an example application (which will lead up to further discussion), consider

\[ N(t,A) = \lambda\{s : X_s \in A, s \in [0,t]\}, \]

understood as the “amount of time, \(X_s\) is in \(A\)” (\(\lambda\) is the Lebesgue measure which is just the length of the set in the traditional sense \(\lambda [0,t) = t\)). Then by Birkoff’s we have that

\[ \lim_{t\rightarrow \infty} \frac{N(t,A)}{t} = \Pr[X \in A] = \mu(A), \]

which says that the amount of time spent in set \(A\) is equal to the probability that any path might be in \(A\) if we wait long enough (basically forget the limit and approximate).6 7

6 Under ergodicity, we see that if \(\mu(J)= 1\) then \(\Pr[X \in A] = \Pr[X \in A \cap J]\).

7 An informal, view of Birkoffs essentially indicates that if we scramble time and take the average under permutation (sample time average) and treat it as if it was from an ensemble (sample population average), the usual law of large numbers for sample means holds \[ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(X_s)\dd s &\approx \frac{1}{T} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} f(X_{s_j}) \Delta s_j \\ &= \frac{1}{T} \sum_{j=0}^{T-1} f(X_{s_{\pi(j)}}) \Delta s_{\pi(j)} \\ &\approx \E[f(X_1)] \end{align*} \] where \(\pi (\cdot)\) is a permutation of all possible rational numbers \(s_j \in \Q \cap [0,T]\) (note: we are considering a countably dense set of points), where we may assume for instance that we are taking “finer” partitions as \(N\uparrow \infty\); we can just take \(\Delta s_i = 1/N\) in the simplest case.

Extentions of Birkoff

In Ergodic theory, what you can discuss are things called Ergodic Components. Basically say you have spaces \((T,J_1,J_2)\) where these spaces don’t connect to one another except from \(T \rightarrow J_j\) (\(T\) is the “transitive space”, basically you eventually leave, unless the process is ugly) and once in \(J_j\) you never leave (at least not in probability). These \(J_j\) could be absorbing states, or might be richer sets, but at the end of the day, instead of using Birkhoff’s in the traditional sense, we can extend this to the Birkhoff-Khinchin which augments Birkhoff as follows8, again defining \(N(t,A) = \lambda\{s : X_s \in A, s \in [0,t]\}\),

8 Importantly, any ergodic measure \(\mu\) can be represented as a decomposition over its ergodic components, with \[ \mu = \rho \mu^{J_1} + (1-\rho)\mu^{J_2} \] where \(\rho = \Pr[X_s \text{ is eventually in } J_1]\) (note: the transient part is missing; there is no \(\mu^T\), as with probability \(1\) all paths eventually leave \(T\)). This extends to more general parameterizations, where for ergodic components identified with parameter \(\theta \in\Theta\) \[ \mu(A) = \int_\Theta \mu^{\theta}(A) \dd \rho(\theta). \]

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X)| J_j] = \pr{\lim_{T\rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{N(T,J_j)} \int_{0}^{T} f(X_s)\mathbb{I}_{X_s \in J_j} \dd s} \quad \text{a.s.} \]

which complicated expression just indicating that conditioned on being in \(J_j\), then Birkhoff holds with \(\mu^{J_j}\) (if you’re in an Ergodic component \(\implies\) you’re Ergodic!). This identifies the asymptotic quantity itself as random variables, specifically9 \[ \begin{align*} \lim_{T\rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^{T} f(X_s)\dd s &= \mathbb{E}[f(X)| J_j] \\ &\text{w.p} \quad\Pr[X_s \ \text{eventually in } J_j] \end{align*} \]

9 We remember here that \(N(t,A)/t \rightarrow \Pr[X\in A]\). Defining \[ \cbr{Y_s : s \in [0,N(T,J_j)]} =\mathrm{Join}\cbr{X_s : X_s \in J_j, s \in [0,T]} \] as a simply reordering/joining of the process \(X_s\) such that that \(X_s \in J_j\); which can approximate in as having times \(s \in [0,T\Pr[X\in J_j]]\); a change of variables in time induces the same space $[0,T]. By definition, we observe that \(\E[f(Y_s)/\Pr[X \in J_j]] = \E[\cbr{X_s | X_s \in J_j}\). We therefore find the stated fact (in approximation): \[ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{N(T,J_j)} \int_{0}^{T} f(X_s)\mathbb{I}_{X_s \in J_j} \dd s &= \frac{1}{N(T,J_j)} \int_{0}^{N(T,J_j)} f(X_s)\mathbb{I}_{X_s \in J_j} \dd s \dd s \\ &\approx \frac{1}{T\Pr[X\in J_j]} \int_{0}^{T\Pr[X\in J_j]} f(Y_s) \dd s \\ &\approx \E[f(X)|\mathcal{I_j}] \end{align*} \]

Once I say “any point” Ergodicity tells me that there are an overwelmingly infinite number of points after a large enough \(T\) that are essentially indistinguishable from one another (in the grand scheme of things - \(L_1\) convergence sense) and therefore this average should look like an ensemble average; getting closer the larger \(N\) is as it relates to \(T\) (size \(T = \lambda[0,T) < \lambda[T,\infty) = \infty, \implies\) “uniform sampling” of points for my average gives \(0\) points coming from \([0,T]\) with probability 1).

Commentary

These prior statements deal with the “long-time” dynamics of this process \(f(X_s)\), presuming that the variability of averages are vanishing. In practice however, this observation is just that the error of our time averages is “small enough” after some large time \(T\) with respect to our ensemble average and that they are consistent across paths (tending to the same quantity for most paths). Essentially, “our estimates are stable and descriptive”, and “our estimates apply equally to populations, and as well as across an individuals life”: that we may transfer ensemble dynamics to path-wise dynamics. Ergodicity give us information the properties of path ensembles of paths. This information is not restricted to infinite time as required by Birkhoff (\(T\rightarrow\infty)\) but has value also in medium or short (finite) time processes, but depends on how representative the population is for the individual. How “representative” will depend on the distribution of the ensemble, specifically how close this distribution approximates the stationary distribution. Temporally, we will care about how long we have observed the individual and the population, asking whether each has had enough time to explore the space; note that if all individuals behave the same, the more coverage we will have (in time and space) of a single individuals life (which we can think of as being approximated by others “close to them” in the population).

In the rest of these notes I will cover some related concepts and at the end include some questions of consideration (either that they were or are being considered by the author). We will begin with a small off-shoot to talking about mixing times, which will attempt to give an answer to “have we watched/measured the individual long enough?” assuming that the population is stationary and the individual dynamic. Afterwards we will discuss the premise of using ergodicity for decisions. Specifically, we will discuss “growth-rate optimality” which is the premise of Ergodicity Economics. This will be the primary (potentially temporary) interest of these notes, attempting to clarify and account for related concepts; as well as being a dumping ground for some superficial analysis.

Convergence rates and Mixing Times

Definition 5 We define the \(\epsilon > 0\) mixing time for measure \(\mu\) as \[ t_\varepsilon =\inf\cbr{t\ge 0:\sup_{\omega\in \Omega} \sup_{Q\in \Omega} \abs{\mu_t^{\omega}(Q) - \mu(Q)}\le \varepsilon}. \]

Mixing times are often fairly difficult to work with (having a rather complicated definition, and require sometimes unclear coupling arguments), but they are a useful measure of how quick a distribution \(\mu_s^{\omega}\) starting at any point \(\omega\in\Omega\) will well approximate the stationary measure \(\mu\). Reiterating, this is still about the ensemble, as a measurement of the uniform worst case across ensembles of possible paths. As such it give us, up to discretization error, a good answer to how quickly a population distributed over \(A\) with ensemble path distribution \(\mu_s^A\) will approximate the stationary distribution \(\mu\).10

10 Here, if \(A\sim \nu\) is a random set (point process), we can represent \(\mu_s^A = \nu \mu_{s}^{\omega^A}\) with \(\omega^A\) identifying elements of the point process \(A\). Here we see that by Jensen’s inequality \[ \begin{align*} \sup_{Q\in\Omega}\abs{\mu_s^A(Q) - \mu(Q)} &\le \E_A \br{\sup_{Q\in\Omega}\abs{\mu_s^{\omega^A}(Q) - \mu(Q)}} \\ &\le \E_A \br{\sup_{\omega\in \Omega} \sup_{Q\in \Omega} \abs{\mu_t^{\omega}(Q) - \mu(Q)}} \\ &= \sup_{\omega\in \Omega} \sup_{Q\in \Omega} \abs{\mu_t^{\omega}(Q) - \mu(Q)} \end{align*} \] and so the mixing time is \(\le t_\varepsilon\); again, up to the discretization error treating \(A\) as an observed quantity.

11 Both this expression, and the expression of mixing time, are related to questions about the convergence of the empirical process associated to the random variable; for later it may be interesting to consider uniform LLN/CLT/etc.

How much data we actually have is important, and this \(t_\varepsilon\) quantity is saying that only really for \(t> t_\varepsilon\) can we consider the observed ensemble a representative sample for the distribution. This still however leaves out the question of how close our specific time average is to the ensemble and population average. To answer this, deliberately non-asymptotically, we need to ask about convergence rates, i.e. for what \(r_n\downarrow 0\) \[ \abs{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T}f\pr{X_t} - \E[f(X)]} = O_p\pr{r_T} \] and the indicated time \(T\) such that this approximation is small enough (say smaller than \(\varepsilon>0\)). 11 We are interested here in a study of “large deviations”, and bounds such as Chernoff, Hoeffding, etc. For reversible ergodic markov chains CLT and non-asymtpotic bound exist for the time average. (Lezaud 1998; Geyer 1992)

Growth-rate/Ergodic Transforms

Definition 6 \(v:\R_+\times\Omega \rightarrow \Omega\) is the growth-rate transform of a process \(X_t\), if it exists is the one such that \(v_t(X_t) = v(t,X_t)\) has ergodic increments \(\Delta v_t (X_t)\)

Note here that to even say “ergodic increments” this implies that we are already considering these increments to be sampled from the associated stationary measure \(\mu\); this is not always made formal or clear in discussion. This stationarity further supposes that the “drift” of \(\Delta v_t (X_t)\), is constant with respect to time \[ \E[\Delta v_t(X_t)|X_t = x] = g(x) \] and further supposes that the noise of \(\Delta v_t (X_t)\) is stationary (again possibly depending on position, but not time). We must note again as well that this is an ensemble property, not a path property.

From Ito’s lemma12, in the simplest case of absolute continutity with respect to a brownian motion \(B_t\), we are saying that \(X_t = f_t(Y_t)\) where \(Y_t\) has ergodic increments and \[ \dd Y_t = g(Y_t)\dd t + \sigma(Y_t)\dd B_t \] where therefore we see \(v_t\) right inverse of \(f_t\). Importantly, but never discussed is that this transform with respect to might \(X_t\) might be valid even for \(X_t\) with non-stationary growth independent our premised \(Y_t\). For instance we may simply consider \[ X_t = e^{Y_t - \int_{0}^t \lambda(t)\dd t} \doteq e^{Y_t - \Lambda_t} \] for non constant \(\lambda(t)\).

A specialization of this idea is what is primary considered in Ergodicity Economics.

Definition 7 \(\ell:\R_+\times\Omega \rightarrow \Omega\) is the linearization of a process \(X_t\), if it exists is the one such that \(\ell_t(X_t) = \ell(t,X_t)\) has ergodic increments \(\Delta v_t (X_t)\) such that \(\E[\Delta v_t (X_t)|X_t = x] = g\) where \(g\) is a constant.

This heavily simplifes Birkoffs Theorem in the Feller case (strong markov; where this operation in the SDE sense makes sense) into \[ \lim_{T\uparrow\infty} \frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^T \dd Y_t = \lim_{h\downarrow0}\E\br{\frac{Y_{t+h}-Y_t}{h} \Bigg |Y_t = y } = g. \]

Poisson equation and Harmonic transforms

By Ito’s lemma finding an ergodic transformation comes down locally, with \[ \begin{align*} \dd X_t &= \xi_t(X_t)\dd t +\rho_t(X_t) \dd B_t \end{align*} \]

Finite Time Games

Most games will be structure as the choice between two or more wealth dynamics. Consider for now simply:

\[ X_s = x_0 e^{Z_t}, \quad Y_t = y_0 e^{W_t} \]

whose whose rate increments \(\Delta Z_t\) and \(\Delta W_t\) are ergodic, with stationary increments, having time-averages in expectation \(g_X\) and \(g_Y\), and where \(x_0\) and \(y_0\) are initial values of wealth.

In the short time (I do not force you to play forever), the adminstrator of this game may be able to “grease the wheel” and bribe you into the worse long-run gamble with enough money out-front (\(x_0, y_0\)). However Asymptotic Growth-Rate Maximization, shortened as AGRM, as would be the recommendation of applying Birkhoff, may give you the wrong answer under finite time horizons.

One-Time Bets: Stochastic precedence is trivial

We will consider one-time bets under linear and multiplicative growth. The important observation is that you must live with the outcome, and therefore the “game” doesn’t truly stop until one dies (a random, finite time horizon). At a time \(\tau\), you are allowed to choose opt-in to the game \(G_\tau\). In the multiplicative case, under the premise of stochastic precedence, we consider the time \(\tau\) relation \[ \begin{align*} \Pr[X_{\tau}^{G} > X_\tau] \doteq \Pr[X_\tau G_\tau > X_\tau], \end{align*} \]

Clearly, choose to play if its a favorable bet, i.e. \(G_\tau\) is likely greater than \(1\). This criteria does not change even when considering \(X_{\tau + T}\), \[ \begin{align*} \Pr[X_{\tau+T}^{G} > X_{\tau +T}] &\doteq \Pr[(X_\tau G_\tau)\frac{X_{\tau+T}}{X_\tau} > X_{\tau+T}] \\ &= \Pr[ G_\tau> 1] \end{align*} \] Essentially the same result will be true under any additive relation of the bet \[ \begin{align*} \Pr[X_{\tau+T}^{G} > X_{\tau +T}] &\doteq \Pr[(X_\tau + G_\tau)\frac{X_{\tau+T}}{X_\tau} > X_{\tau+T}] \\ &= \Pr\br{\frac{X_\tau +G_\tau}{X_\tau}>1} \\ &= \Pr\br{G_\tau>0} \end{align*} \] where, though the decision at first appears relative to your current wealth at time time \(\tau\), it is again also trivially a question of whether \(G_\tau\) tends to be positive.
